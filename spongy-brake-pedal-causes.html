Spongy Brake Pedal: Causes and What to Check

By Dima · Published November 1, 2026 · Updated November 5, 2026 · 4 min read

Quick answer

A spongy brake pedal usually means air in the lines, old or contaminated brake fluid, a leak, swollen hoses or a worn master cylinder. Check the fluid level and colour, look for wet marks near the wheels and see whether the pedal sinks under steady pressure. If it sinks to the floor or the car doesn't slow normally, stop driving and have it towed.

A brake pedal should feel firm and consistent. If yours feels soft, spongy or sinks further than it used to, don't ignore it.

Safety note. If the pedal goes to the floor, sinks under steady pressure or the car doesn't slow normally, stop driving and have it towed to a workshop.

Most common causes

Air in the brake lines

Air compresses and fluid doesn't. If air has got into the system, usually after a repair, the pedal feels soft and often firms up if you pump it. The fix is bleeding the brakes.

Old, moisture-filled brake fluid

As brake fluid absorbs water, its boiling point drops and the pedal can go soft after hard braking. Check when it was last changed. Two years is a common interval.

A fluid leak

A leak from a hose, caliper, brake line or master cylinder lets pressure escape. Look for wet patches near the wheels, under the car and around the reservoir, and check the fluid level.

Swollen or aged brake hoses

Rubber flexible hoses can swell internally with age, absorbing pressure instead of passing it on. They can look fine from outside.

Worn master cylinder

If the pedal slowly sinks to the floor with steady pressure but you can't find a leak, the master cylinder's internal seals may be worn.

Pads, calipers or drums

Very worn pads, sticking calipers or drum brakes that are out of adjustment can all add travel and a vague feel.

Brake booster problems

A faulty booster usually makes the pedal feel hard, not soft, but it's worth knowing the difference.

Old fluid is a frequent cause. See How Often to Change Brake Fluid and How to Check It. If you spot a puddle, Fluid Leaking Under Your Car helps you identify it.

Quick checks you can do safely

  1. Park on level ground and check the brake fluid level against MIN and MAX.
  2. Look at the fluid. Dark brown means old.
  3. Look behind each wheel and at the hoses for wet marks or bulges.
  4. With the engine off, pump the pedal a few times. It should go hard. Then hold light pressure and start the engine: the pedal should drop slightly.
  5. Press and hold the pedal for 30 seconds. It shouldn't slowly sink.

What the symptoms suggest

  • Pumping makes it firmer: likely air or very old fluid.
  • Slowly sinks while held: suspect a leak or the master cylinder.
  • Low fluid level: a leak, or very worn pads. Find out which.
  • Soft only after hard braking: fluid is likely overheating. Check its age.

Worn pads or discs add travel and noise too; see Brake Pads vs Rotors and Grinding Noise When Braking.

Fixing it

Most fixes involve bleeding the system or replacing a hose or caliper. These are the jobs where mistakes matter, so unless you're confident with brakes, have a workshop deal with it. Never top up fluid and carry on without finding out why it was low.

FAQ

Why does my brake pedal feel spongy?

Air in the lines, old brake fluid, a leak, swollen hoses or a worn master cylinder are the usual causes.

Is a spongy brake pedal dangerous?

Yes. A soft pedal can mean reduced braking. If it sinks to the floor, don't drive.

Will bleeding the brakes fix a spongy pedal?

If air or old fluid is the cause, usually yes. It won't fix a leak or a worn master cylinder.

Why is my brake pedal soft only sometimes?

Often the fluid overheats after hard braking, or air moves around in the system. Check the fluid's age and condition.
